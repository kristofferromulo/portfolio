Academic experimental research UPLB · 2026 Best Undergraduate Thesis in Economics

Assessing grading bias from AI disclosure. What a label does to judgment.

UP asks students to disclose when they use AI. I tested whether that disclosure changes how the same essay is graded and trusted. It did, and it cost the strongest work the most.

Role
Sole author. Adviser: Richard B. Daite
Design
2 × 2 within-rater factorial vignette experiment
Participants
102 BS Economics students; 8 faculty for the benchmark
Data
408 essay ratings, collected February 2026 in Qualtrics
Analysis
OLS with respondent-clustered standard errors, Stata 18
Repository record ↗ Survey preview ↗ Full text is limited to UP accounts
01

Context

Generative AI makes it harder to see how much of a submission reflects a student's own effort. The University of the Philippines responded with guidelines in 2023 that ask students to disclose AI use.

Economics has a name for what can go wrong next. A grade is a signal of ability the grader can't observe directly (Spence 1973), and when one side knows less about quality, it tends to discount (Akerlof 1970). An AI label adds new information to that exchange. It could inform the grade, or it could bias it.

02

Question

Do AI disclosure labels change how evaluators grade the same student work, and how credible they find it?

  1. Objective 1Measure the size and direction of the label's effect on grades and perceived credibility.
  2. Objective 2Check whether the effect holds after adjusting for how lenient each rater is against a faculty benchmark.

Five hypotheses followed: the label lowers grades (H1), stronger essays earn more (H2), the label's effect depends on quality (H3), rater leniency moves grades (H4), and the penalty persists after all of these (H5).

03

Design

Every rater graded four short economics essays: a weaker and a stronger version on rice price caps, and the same on sin taxes. The AI label was randomized separately for each essay, so the same text carried the label for some raters and not for others. Essay order was randomized too.

Because each person rated all four essays, every rater served as their own comparison. Standard errors are clustered by rater to account for the repeated ratings.

04

Process

  1. 01

    Build the stimuli

    Four essay snippets under 120 words, written with GPT-4. Quality differed in economic reasoning, depth, evidence, organization and clarity, not just readability.

  2. 02

    Pilot the quality gap

    Respondents outside the main sample graded the essays without labels. Wave 1 missed my criteria, so I revised the prompts and stimuli. Wave 2 passed.

  3. 03

    Set a faculty benchmark

    Eight of ten eligible Department of Economics faculty graded a common anchor essay with an analytic rubric. Their mean of 81 became the reference for each rater's leniency.

  4. 04

    Recruit with consent

    Invitations went through the Office of the College Secretary. The survey opened on informed consent, and only students who agreed continued.

  5. 05

    Run the task

    Each student graded the anchor essay, then the four essays in random order. Each essay got a 0–100 grade, a 1–5 credibility rating and an understanding check.

  6. 06

    Debrief, clean, estimate

    A debrief explained the randomization. Raffle entries went through a separate form to keep responses anonymous. I coded the data in protected Google Sheets, dropped incomplete responses and failed checks, and estimated the models in Stata 18.

05

Findings

Takeaway

Raters recognized quality: stronger essays scored about 13.5 points higher. The AI label then took back the most from those same essays, about 9.3 points, against 3.6 for weaker ones.

Credibility moved more than grades. The label lowered perceived credibility even for weaker essays (−0.85 on a five-point scale, p < .001), where the grade barely moved. Without the leniency control the pattern holds: −2.37 for weaker essays (p = .246) and an interaction of −6.72 (p = .011).

H1 · Label lowers grades

Small and marginal for weaker essays; larger and significant for stronger ones.

Partially supported
H2 · Quality raises grades

Positive and significant in every grade model.

Supported
H3 · Effect depends on quality

The interaction shows a larger penalty at high quality.

Supported
H4 · Leniency moves grades

More lenient raters gave higher grades.

Supported
H5 · Penalty survives controls

Clearest in the high-quality condition.

Conditionally supported
06

Interpretation

The label didn't subtract a flat amount. It took the most from the essays that had earned the most. One reading, which the thesis puts forward, is that raters credited part of a strong essay's quality to the tool rather than the student.

The credibility results point the same way. Raters graded weaker labeled essays about the same as unlabeled ones, but trusted them less. The label changed what the work signaled as well as how it scored.

07

Implications

Disclosure supports transparency, but on its own it can create a conditional penalty for good work. The thesis recommends bias-informed disclosure policies and grader calibration, so that an honest label doesn't cost students credit their work earned.

Why it matters for digital work

Interfaces are full of labels like "AI-generated", "sponsored" and "verified". Readers interpret them as signals, and this study is a template for testing one before it ships.

08

Limits

  • One department, with students as raters. Faculty may grade differently.
  • The essays were short, constructed snippets written with GPT-4, not real coursework.
  • A vignette task is not a real grading session.
  • Leniency calibration rests on a single benchmark essay.

The next study the thesis proposes: sample faculty, and use stimuli drawn from actual grading.

01

Context

OlyNation was a distributor community for a wellness-technology company. The team site, olynation.team, was its public front. Onboarding happened in GoHighLevel: a landing page, a form and a thank-you page, followed by automated emails and materials. The goal was to bring new members all the way into membership.

02

Problem

People weren't reaching the next step.

GoHighLevel showed where the funnel lost them, and the losses changed with the order of the presentation videos. What the numbers couldn't say was why.

03

My role

Mine
  • The team website, designed and built on my own in Wix
  • The look of the funnel pages and the campaign
  • The new OlyNation identity and its brand guide
  • Design changes in response to the evidence
With others
  • The Mint team handled production legwork
  • Group leaders collected and relayed member feedback
04

Evidence

05

Changes

  • Video orderSwitched between sequences and kept the one GoHighLevel showed losing fewer people.
  • Content flowReordered the page so each section leads to the next.
  • HierarchyMade the content and the call to action read in order of importance.
  • JourneySimplified the path from first visit to sign-up.
  • LanguagesAdded support for other languages, which members had asked for.
  • IdentityReplaced the dated OlyLife-derived look with an OlyNation identity I designed.
Rules I set in the guide
  • Body text in a light weight, tracked tight, in grey.
  • Headings in a regular weight, never bold.
  • Emphasis by brightness: white text, or the green gradient for the strongest emphasis.
  • Header gradients always run white above, green below.
  • The logo mark can return as a graphic element, with corners neither too round nor too sharp.
06

Outcome

I can't report numbers. The GoHighLevel account went with the ownership transfer in May 2026, and we didn't keep exports in a fast-moving schedule. What I can say is which way each decision went: the version kept was the one the step data showed losing fewer people.

After the transfer, the team moved to OlyNation Prime, and onboarding moved into Rapid Prime Connect, an app built to run and track the same journey in one place. I now design its landing pages.

07

Limits

  • The video orders ran one after another, not at the same time. Changes in traffic, timing or audience could explain part of any difference.
  • Feedback reached me second-hand, filtered through leaders.
  • Without saved figures, the size of the improvement can't be checked.
08

Next time

  • Split, don't switchSend traffic to both versions at the same time.
  • Define drop-off firstWrite down what counts as leaving each step before launch.
  • Keep snapshotsSave the dashboard before and after every change.
  • Hear it first-handTalk to a handful of new members directly.
