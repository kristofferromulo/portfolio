02 · Approach

Approach

How evidence turns into design: name the decision, use the evidence that can answer it, and change the design where it points.

TL;DR

Every case follows the same four steps, starting with the decision.

  1. 01

    Ask the question

    Name the decision that depends on the answer, like which step to fix first.

  2. 02

    Pick the evidence

    Step data for where people stop, conversations for why, experiments for whether a change makes the difference.

  3. 03

    Find the pattern

    Group what I see into a few patterns, and note how strong each one is.

  4. 04

    Design, then check

    Make the change, then check that it held: in a team walkthrough, on the live site, or in the next round of data.

Academic experimental research UPLB · 2026 Best Undergraduate Thesis in Economics

Assessing grading bias from AI disclosure. What a label does to judgment.

UP asks students to disclose when they use AI. I tested whether that disclosure changes how the same essay is graded and trusted. It did, and it cost the strongest work the most.

Role
Sole author. Adviser: Richard B. Daite
Design
2 × 2 within-rater factorial vignette experiment
Participants
102 BS Economics students; 8 faculty for the benchmark
Data
408 essay ratings, collected February 2026 in Qualtrics
Analysis
OLS with respondent-clustered standard errors, Stata 18
Repository record ↗ Survey preview ↗ Full text is limited to UP accounts

Brief

In plain words

I ran an experiment to test whether an AI-use label changes how people grade the same piece of work.

What this case shows
  • Research-trained

    I designed, piloted and analyzed it myself.

  • Understands digital experiences

    Labels like "AI-generated" shape judgment online, too.

  • Evidence into improvements

    It ends with practical recommendations for policy and grading.

Terms you'll hear
AI-use label
A note saying AI helped write it.
2 × 2 design
Weaker or stronger essay, label on or off.
Within-rater
Everyone graded all four versions.
Credibility
How trustworthy it felt, from 1 to 5.
p-value
Below .05: unlikely to be chance.
01

Context

TL;DR

The University of the Philippines now asks students to disclose when they use AI, which raises a question: can that label alone change how their work is judged?

Generative AI makes it harder to see how much of a submission reflects a student's own effort. The University of the Philippines responded with guidelines in 2023 that ask students to disclose AI use.

Economics has a name for what can go wrong next. A grade is a signal of ability the grader can't observe directly (Spence 1973), and when one side knows less about quality, it tends to discount (Akerlof 1970). An AI label adds new information to that exchange. It could inform the grade, or it could bias it.

02

Question

Do AI disclosure labels change how evaluators grade the same student work, and how credible they find it?

TL;DR

I measured both the grade and how much graders trusted the work, because the two don't always move together.

  1. Objective 1Measure the size and direction of the label's effect on grades and perceived credibility.
  2. Objective 2Check whether the effect holds after adjusting for how lenient each rater is against a faculty benchmark.

Five hypotheses followed: the label lowers grades (H1), stronger essays earn more (H2), the label's effect depends on quality (H3), rater leniency moves grades (H4), and the penalty persists after all of these (H5).

03

Design

TL;DR

I built the test around short essays I prepared myself, so every grader saw identical work and only the AI label and the essay's quality changed.

Every rater graded four short economics essays: a weaker and a stronger version on rice price caps, and the same on sin taxes. The AI label was randomized separately for each essay, so the same text carried the label for some raters and not for others. Essay order was randomized too.

Because each person rated all four essays, every rater served as their own comparison. Standard errors are clustered by rater to account for the repeated ratings.

04

Process

TL;DR

I ran a pilot first to check that graders could tell the weaker essays from the stronger ones, and rewrote them when the first try fell short.

  1. 01

    Build the stimuli

    Four essay snippets under 120 words, written with GPT-4. Quality differed in economic reasoning, depth, evidence, organization and clarity, not just readability.

  2. 02

    Pilot the quality gap

    Respondents outside the main sample graded the essays without labels. Wave 1 missed my criteria, so I revised the prompts and stimuli. Wave 2 passed.

  3. 03

    Set a faculty benchmark

    Eight of ten eligible Department of Economics faculty graded a common anchor essay with an analytic rubric. Their mean of 81 became the reference for each rater's leniency.

  4. 04

    Recruit with consent

    Invitations went through the Office of the College Secretary. The survey opened on informed consent, and only students who agreed continued.

  5. 05

    Run the task

    Each student graded the anchor essay, then the four essays in random order. Each essay got a 0–100 grade, a 1–5 credibility rating and an understanding check.

  6. 06

    Debrief, clean, estimate

    A debrief explained the randomization. Raffle entries went through a separate form to keep responses anonymous. I coded the data in protected Google Sheets, dropped incomplete responses and failed checks, and estimated the models in Stata 18.

05

Findings

TL;DR

The AI label cost the stronger essays about nine points and lowered trust in weaker and stronger essays alike.

Takeaway

Raters recognized quality: stronger essays scored about 13.5 points higher. The AI label then took back the most from those same essays, about 9.3 points, against 3.6 for weaker ones.

Credibility moved more than grades. The label lowered perceived credibility even for weaker essays (−0.85 on a five-point scale, p < .001), where the grade barely moved. Without the leniency control the pattern holds: −2.37 for weaker essays (p = .246) and an interaction of −6.72 (p = .011).

H1 · Label lowers grades

Small and marginal for weaker essays; larger and significant for stronger ones.

Partially supported
H2 · Quality raises grades

Positive and significant in every grade model.

Supported
H3 · Effect depends on quality

The interaction shows a larger penalty at high quality.

Supported
H4 · Leniency moves grades

More lenient raters gave higher grades.

Supported
H5 · Penalty survives controls

Clearest in the high-quality condition.

Conditionally supported
06

Interpretation

TL;DR

The better the essay, the more the AI label cost it.

The label didn't subtract a flat amount. It took the most from the essays that had earned the most. One reading, which the thesis puts forward, is that raters credited part of a strong essay's quality to the tool rather than the student.

The credibility results point the same way. Raters graded weaker labeled essays about the same as unlabeled ones, but trusted them less. The label changed what the work signaled as well as how it scored.

07

Implications

TL;DR

Labels like "AI-generated" now appear across digital products, so they're worth testing before launch in case they penalize good work there too.

Disclosure supports transparency, but on its own it can create a conditional penalty for good work. The thesis recommends bias-informed disclosure policies and grader calibration, so that an honest label doesn't cost students credit their work earned.

Why it matters for digital work

Interfaces are full of labels like "AI-generated", "sponsored" and "verified". Readers interpret them as signals, and this study is a template for testing one before it ships.

08

Limits

TL;DR

The study covered one department and short test essays, so testing with faculty comes next.

  • One department, with students as raters. Faculty may grade differently.
  • The essays were short, constructed snippets written with GPT-4, not real coursework.
  • A vignette task is not a real grading session.
  • Leniency calibration rests on a single benchmark essay.

The next study the thesis proposes: sample faculty, and use stimuli drawn from actual grading.

OlyNation, a distributor community I worked on at Mint Digital, was losing people during sign-up, and I found out where and why.

01

Context

TL;DR

New members started on the team site I built, then went through a five-step sign-up funnel.

OlyNation was a distributor community for a wellness-technology company. The team site, olynation.team, was its public front. Onboarding happened in GoHighLevel: a landing page, a form and a thank-you page, followed by automated emails and materials. The goal was to bring new members all the way into membership.

02

Problem

New members weren't making it to the next sign-up step.

TL;DR

We could see where people left, but not why.

GoHighLevel showed where the funnel lost them, and the losses changed with the order of the presentation videos. What the numbers couldn't say was why.

03

My role

TL;DR

I designed, built and fixed the site, while the team helped produce and test it.

Mine
  • The team website, designed and built on my own in Wix
  • The look of the funnel pages and the campaign
  • The new OlyNation identity and its brand guide
  • Design changes in response to the evidence
  • Fixes for issues found in walkthroughs, reports and my own checks
  • Requested updates across both GoHighLevel and Wix
With others
  • The Mint team handled production legwork
  • Group leaders collected member feedback and shared it with me, partly on Zoom calls
  • Teammates and leaders tested the site in task-based walkthroughs before activation events
  • Our project manager forwarded reports and update requests
04

Evidence

TL;DR

Because no single source could explain the drop-off, I used four: data showed where people left, conversations with group leaders suggested why, and team testing and user reports showed what broke.

I kept four kinds of evidence apart, because each answers a different question.

05

Changes

TL;DR

The evidence led to six design changes, from the order of the presentation videos to a new visual identity.

  • Video orderI tried the presentation videos in different orders and kept the one that lost fewer people.
  • Content flowI reordered the page so each section leads into the next.
  • HierarchyI arranged the content and the main button in order of importance.
  • JourneyI simplified the path from first visit to sign-up.
  • LanguagesI added other languages, which members had asked for.
  • IdentityI replaced the dated look borrowed from OlyLife, the parent brand, with a new OlyNation identity.

Fixes from reports and checks, with their source

On mobile, the resource library opened on all languages and led with Hindi content, though English was the intended default

Source

A group leader's report, forwarded by our project manager. The library was about to be used in activation calls and events.

What I changed

Own check · Oct 2025

Source

Fixed the tools page, and finished the product update on both platforms the evening it was requested.

Rules I set in the guide
  • Body text in a light weight, tracked tight, in grey.
  • Headings in a regular weight, never bold.
  • Emphasis by brightness: white text, or the green gradient for the strongest emphasis.
  • Header gradients always run white above, green below.
  • The logo mark can return as a graphic element, with corners neither too round nor too sharp.
06

Outcome

TL;DR

I didn't save the numbers at the time, because I hadn't yet started treating design work as research.

I can't report numbers. The GoHighLevel account went to the new owners in May 2026, and I hadn't kept exports, because at the time I saw this as design work rather than research. What I can say is which way each decision went: the version kept was the one the step data showed losing fewer people.

After the transfer, the team moved to OlyNation Prime, and onboarding moved into Rapid Prime Connect, an app built to run and track the same journey in one place. I now design its landing pages.

07

Limits

TL;DR

The versions ran one after another rather than side by side, so I don't claim how big the improvement was.

  • The video orders ran one after another, not at the same time. Changes in traffic, timing or audience could explain part of any difference.
  • Member feedback reached me second-hand, through leaders and our project manager, so I can't say how many people hit each issue.
  • Walkthroughs were run by teammates and leaders, who know the site better than new members do.
  • Without saved figures, the size of the improvement can't be checked.
08

Learnings

TL;DR

The biggest lessons were to measure from day one and to test with people who are new to the site.

  • Compare side by sideRunning the video orders one after another meant other changes could explain the difference.
  • Define the measure firstDeciding what counts as drop-off before launch keeps every version measured the same way.
  • Save the evidenceLosing the dashboard in the handover taught me to save it before and after every change.
  • Test with new membersOur walkthroughs used people who already knew the site, so they couldn't show how newcomers would manage.

Brief

In plain words

OlyNation Prime is an independent distributor team I design for, and I fixed the steps where new members reported getting stuck on its website.

What this case shows
  • Understands digital experiences

    The problems were about people getting lost between setup steps.

  • Evidence into improvements

    Each fix traces back to an audit finding you can check live.

01

Context

TL;DR

In May 2026, new members had to get through seven setup steps, each on its own page.

OlyNation Prime's website walks new members through getting activated. In May 2026 it did that with a seven-step path: numbered tabs at the top, a page for each step, and Back and Next buttons between them.

02

Question

Where does the activation path confuse or stop people, and what should change first?

03

Who did what

TL;DR

Users reported the problems, our COO collected them into an audit, and I made the fixes.

04

Evidence

TL;DR

The audit listed 14 problems, each with a screenshot, so I could see exactly what users saw.

The audit groups findings under five headings. Each comes with a screenshot of the issue and, for most, a concrete suggestion, such as "Next: Step 7" instead of "Next", or a Home button between Back and Next.

05

Patterns

TL;DR

Most problems came from people losing their place, and one button didn't work at all.

Read together, the 14 findings fall into three patterns.

6

WayfindingWhere am I, and how do I move?

  • No navigation bar
  • No way to jump to a step
  • Step numbers without context
  • Generic "Next" and "Back"
  • Logo didn't go home
  • No footer
3

Broken or unclear actionsWhat happens when I click?

  • "Next: Step 7" didn't respond
  • Buttons and content labels looked the same
  • "Contact the sender" didn't say what happens next
5

Content and fitIs it easy to take in?

  • FAQ answers formed a long wall
  • Two entries both labeled "IPE 1"
  • Too large at 100% zoom
  • Extra whitespace at the bottom
  • Logo layout
Takeaway

Most of the friction was about orientation, not looks. The one hard blocker, a dead "Next: Step 7" button, stopped people just before the final step.

07

Outcome

TL;DR

We didn't track completion numbers, so the live site is the proof.

No completion figures were kept for this round, so I can't put a number on the effect. What can be checked is the site itself: the eight findings visible on it today read as resolved, and the blocker is gone because the path no longer depends on a step-to-step button.

08

Limits

TL;DR

The feedback came second-hand, so I only claim what you can see on the live site.

  • The feedback reached me through the COO, so I can't say how many users raised each issue.
  • The site kept changing after these fixes, so today's version reflects more than this one round.
  • Some findings can't be checked from the live site, and they're listed as such above.
09

Learnings

TL;DR

The main lesson was that feedback is more useful when you count it and re-test the fix.

  • Count the voicesWithout knowing how many people raised each issue, I couldn't tell which problems were most common.
  • Re-test after fixingA fix is only confirmed once new members walk the path again.
  • Measure completionWithout completion numbers, the live site was my only proof of improvement.
  • Date the evidenceDated before-and-after screenshots make every fix easy to check later.
